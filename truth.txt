Anyhow, enough about me. When we are going to coordinate living labs within the Water Mining project, it is important to understand what a living lab actually is and how it is defined. There is no fixed definition of a living lab. There is a wide variety of living labs, since MIT came up with the term in the late 1990s. MIT originally saw a living lab as an environment or test environment full of technology where people were allowed to live for a certain period. In the meantime, the movement has evolved considerably. What we call Living Labs 1.0 has developed into Living Labs 2.0, with many different forms, including urban living labs, research-driven living labs, living test beds, and neighborhood-focused initiatives. Smart cities also use many living lab methodologies.

What all these approaches have in common is that they focus on joint value—creating value for everyone—through co-creation activities, rapid prototyping, short iterative feedback loops, testing, iteration, and eventually scaling up to a larger ecosystem. They do this in a real-life environment, which is the major difference between living labs and test beds, fab labs, maker spaces, or similar environments. I usually explain it this way: when a fab lab invites the user into the lab, a living lab transposes the lab to the user's environment. We most of the time work with technologies and follow a life-cycle approach that includes ideation, co-creation, experimentation, and validation.

A living lab is not something that can be physically grasped. It is not simply a group of people, a building that can be constructed, or an online platform for exchanging ideas. It is the whole of these elements. It is a way of approaching innovation and, in many ways, a way of working and living rather than simply a consortium. All these components are part of the living lab. Despite the wide variety of living labs, there are common elements. First, a living lab operates within a quadruple or quintuple helix environment. The quintuple helix adds the environment as the fifth helix. It involves multiple actors, including government, industry, research, users, and citizens. These living labs share five common elements: orchestration, co-creation, active user involvement, multi-stakeholder participation, and working in a real-life setting through a multi-method approach.
